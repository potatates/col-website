Source: National Business Daily

Microdrama is where AI is reshaping content most dramatically.

At the recent 2026 Tencent Global Digital Ecosystem Summit, microdrama, AI and taking content global were among the hottest topics. As a strategic partner in the Tencent ecosystem, COL Group works with Tencent on compute, model access and content distribution, a collaboration seen as one window into how “platform + content + model” is reshaping popular culture.

During the summit, Ma Tao, General Manager of COL’s Strategic Operations Department and its Brand Department, discussed the changes underway in the industry. Microdrama has grown into a leading audiovisual format by output value, and Chinese stories are reaching the world with far lower barriers to entry. AI is an excellent executor, he said, but it is still people who decide “what to write.”

Over the past year, microdrama has been formally recognized as a mainstream audiovisual format and one of the most active categories of “new popular culture,” with industry output now exceeding that of film. At the same time, AI has sharply cut production costs and timelines, loosening the industry’s established production relationships. How content companies and platform ecosystems divide the work, and who safeguards the quality of a good story, are becoming the more pressing questions behind the microdrama boom overseas.

## A new place in the ecosystem: from selling finished products to building the production chain together

Judging by the messages from the summit, microdrama and AI content remain a key focus of platform ecosystem partnerships.

Platforms need a steady, reliable and scalable supply of content; content companies like COL need channels to reach users and more efficient production tools. Where those needs meet is the practical basis for this round of ecosystem cooperation. COL’s partnership with Tencent has long gone beyond content licensing: from distributing webnovels and microdramas on WeChat Read, Tencent Video and WeChat, to tapping video model capabilities and integrating production tools, the relationship is shifting from transaction to co-creation.

The significance of this shift is that the division of labour in microdrama is being rewritten.

In the past, a content company sold a finished product to a platform, and the relationship ended once the deal was done. Today, AI has standardized and modularized storyboarding, generation and post-production. Platforms provide model capabilities and compute as basic utilities, while content companies provide IP, taste and judgment, with each responsible for different stages of the same chain.

Ma Tao describes AI’s current role as that of an “executor”: it follows orders, goes wherever it is pointed and carries out tasks almost perfectly. The real test becomes deciding what it should execute, and to what standard. In a sense, this also draws the new boundary of the ecosystem: models supply capability, content companies supply judgment.

In his view, what ultimately separates models is not the three classic factors of compute, algorithms and data, but a fourth: the use case, meaning a command of the industry, its fundamentals and its resource ecosystem. Many models later faded away precisely because they lacked one. For a content company, the use case is years of understanding of fiction writing and the microdrama business: what kind of story keeps people reading and what pacing keeps them watching to the end. That kind of experience is hard to capture in a general-purpose model’s training data.

## Rising tolerance: AI enters creative work, but the human touch is the bottom line

Once AI entered the creative process, the first controversy was over content that “reads like AI.” Ma Tao says the industry has reached a consensus: encourage good stories, but not purely AI-generated writing.

“Purely AI-generated text is actually a kind of pollution of human creativity,” he said. Story arcs, characterization and the protagonist’s growth must be built by people: “Without a human touch, it’s finished.” The old benchmark of webnovel authors writing more than 3,000 characters a day has all but disappeared. Give AI a single sentence and it can produce tens of thousands of characters in no time, but whether anyone will keep reading is another matter.

On content platforms, “using AI to beat AI” in screening is becoming standard. AI first flags telltale signs in submissions, such as back-and-forth plot reversals rarely seen in webnovels and piled-up parallel sentences, then prompts editors to step in. Manuscripts that even the editors cannot get through are rejected outright.

In other words, AI both amplifies and filters output.

Yet the pace of technical progress is just as visible. Ma Tao expects readers’ acceptance of AI-assisted writing to keep rising as models improve: “That threshold will definitely keep getting higher.” AI can already write short pieces with sound plots and brisk pacing, though it still falls clearly short on medium-length and long-form works.

Ma Tao was also frank that making money from fiction is actually harder in the AI era: writers compete not only with professional authors but also with ordinary people who use AI well. For creators with long experience, however, using AI to rework plots gives them a real edge. “If you truly dream of writing novels or becoming a writer, I think now is the best time.”

## Lower costs: going global becomes a contest of Chinese imagination

As much lower production costs become the industry norm, the logic of competition is changing. Volume content and premium content are running on separate tracks, and some companies have begun to judge teams on their rate of quality output rather than their rate of hits, since hits cannot be planned.

COL’s approach is to keep refining an end-to-end AI pipeline from script to distribution, backed by its production bases and compute partnerships, with a tilt toward premium content.

Lower costs are also changing how Chinese stories travel abroad. Ma Tao observes that Chinese history, cultivation and science fiction were once hard to export because of cultural barriers and visual effects costs. Now AI can make them highly visual: concepts that were hard to explain in words, such as “what the Tao is, what qi is, what cultivation means,” can be shown in a single shot. Overseas viewers’ interest is shifting from purely emotional dramas toward Chinese cultivation, xianxia and sci-fi stories.

One telling figure: in 2025, COL’s revenue from microdrama and IP derivatives grew 125% year on year, surpassing webnovel revenue for the first time to become its largest revenue source. AI and microdrama are reshaping the revenue structure of the company, and of the industry as a whole.

Seen from the ecosystem’s perspective, this round of change in microdrama is in some ways a microcosm of “new popular culture”: production tools are spreading, creative barriers are falling, participants are expanding from professional studios to ordinary people, and the division of labour among platforms, content companies and creators is being redefined along the way. Only the strongest survive, which is both the reality for companies and the norm for the industry.
